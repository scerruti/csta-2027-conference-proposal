# Acting on AP CS Score Reports: A Hands-On Data-Driven Cycle

## Session Type

Session (1 hour)

## Abstract

In this hands-on session, AP CSA and CSP teachers work through a complete data-driven cycle with their own AP results. Being data-driven means the data identifies where to look, the teacher's experience forms a hypothesis, and new data tests it. Participants turn their score roster into a question, slice and download the Instructional Planning Report to answer it, and combine its test forms with a free, privacy-focused browser tool. In small groups of teachers from different schools who share a weak skill, they treat the place where a weak unit and a weak practice meet as a hypothesis, prioritizing foundational concepts and drawing on each other's experience. Each participant then writes an action plan and builds and schedules an AP Classroom formative assessment to test the hypothesis during the year. Results are expected to arrive around conference time, so the work starts with current data. If you can, bring a laptop and your College Board educator login; participants without access work alongside a partner. Attendees leave with a slicing and download handout, the tool, CSA and CSP worksheets, and a guide to AP Classroom formative assessments. This session aligns with CSTA Teacher Standards 4g (inform instruction through assessment), 4f (plan instruction to foster student understanding), and 3f (participate in CS professional learning communities).

## Learning Goals

Participants will:

- Distinguish data-aware from data-driven instruction, using a working definition: the data identifies where to look, the teacher's experience forms a hypothesis, and new data tests it.
- Form a question from their own Subject Score Roster, or a partner's, and confirm together that the report can answer it.
- Slice and download an Instructional Planning Report to match the question, and combine its test forms with a browser tool.
- Form a hypothesis with colleagues who share a weak skill or an area of interest, stating the evidence before interpreting it and prioritizing earlier units.
- Write an action plan for their top hypothesis, fitted to their adopted curriculum.
- Build and schedule an AP Classroom formative assessment that tests the hypothesis during the year.

## Educational Basis

This session draws on four research-based practices:

- **Data use as a cycle** (Marsh, 2012). In a review of research on interventions that support educators' use of data, Marsh describes data use as a cycle. Data is organized into information, combined with educator expertise to become actionable knowledge, acted on, and evaluated. Participants work through that cycle with their own results. Marsh found that educators most often lack support for moving from knowledge to action, so the session's second half is devoted to planning and building an assessment. She also found that a single training session alone rarely led to data use, so participants leave with the resources to repeat the cycle each year.
- **Structured collaboration across schools** (Marsh, 2012). Among the promising practices Marsh identifies are opportunities for educators from different schools to collaborate, and norms that make data discussions safe and productive. Skill groups bring together teachers from different schools who share a weak skill. Each discussion states the evidence before anyone interprets it, so the conversation stays on the data rather than on judging teachers.
- **Formative assessment** (Black & Wiliam, 1998). Assessment is formative only when its evidence is used to adapt teaching. AP score reports arrive after the course ends; an AP Classroom formative assessment provides evidence during the year, while there is still time to respond. Here the assessment tests a hypothesis about one skill rather than serving as a grade.
- **Prioritizing foundational concepts** (Cabo, 2015). In a first college Java course, performance on foundational concepts such as assignment, repetition, and syntax predicted students' ability to write working programs, and a gap in any one concept held students back. AP CSA teaches the same language and many of the same concepts, so participants prioritize earlier units.

The collaborative step draws on two ideas from the Data Wise Improvement Process (Harvard Graduate School of Education): examining practice alongside evidence of student learning, and stating evidence before interpreting it.

## Standards Alignment

- **CSTA 4g, Inform instruction through assessment:** Notice and Investigate (0:03–0:26) use participants' own AP results to decide where to focus. Test (0:44–0:56) builds and schedules an AP Classroom formative assessment that provides evidence to adjust instruction during the year.
- **CSTA 4f, Plan instruction to foster student understanding:** Hypothesize (0:26–0:38) locates the likely gap in understanding and prioritizes foundational concepts, and Plan (0:38–0:44) designs the instructional change.
- **CSTA 3f, Participate in CS professional learning communities:** skill groups of teachers from different schools work together from the sort through Test (0:23–0:56), sharing practice and assessment searches, and the close (0:56–1:00) makes the case for continuing that work in a PLC or community of practice.

## Session Outline

### 0:00-0:03 | Log In and Start

- Everyone opens a laptop and logs in to the College Board score reporting site while the presenter talks. Participants without access find a partner who has it.
- The hour's promise: "By the end of this session you will have been data-driven with your own results." Data-driven means the data identifies where to look, your experience forms a hypothesis, and new data tests it.
- Introduce the three stages (Notice, Investigate, Respond).

### 0:03-0:05 | Notice: Look

- Open the Subject Score Roster: score distribution, mean, and percentage of scores 3 or higher, by section and against state and global peers.

### 0:05-0:10 | Notice: Ask

- Write a question the data could answer, such as "How do I get more students to pass?" or "How could my period 4 students earn a 5?" Sample questions and anti-questions on the handout help.
- Engagement: partners check that each question can be answered by slicing the report. Latecomers join a pair here and shadow their partner through the data steps.
- Discussion: Why start with a question instead of opening the detailed report?

### 0:10-0:18 | Investigate: Slice and Download

- Filter the Instructional Planning Report by score band and/or section to match the question (for example, only the 2s, since 3 is passing), then download it. One download contains every test form.
- Engagement: partners confirm that the downloaded slice matches the question, and help each other with problems in the portal.

### 0:18-0:23 | Investigate: Combine

- Load the report into a free, privacy-focused browser tool. It combines the test forms into two tables, units and practices, compared with global performance; reports never leave the laptop. The combined view is an approximation, good enough to decide where to look.
- Find the weakest unit and practice, and locate where they meet on the CSA or CSP Data Deep Dive matrix. Prefer earlier units, since later units build on them.
- College Board also recommends comparing two slices (such as 2s against 3s and above) as a next step to try on your own.

### 0:23-0:26 | Sort Into Skill Groups

- Signs mark each course's practices. Participants go to their weakest practice. Crowded groups split into groups of three or four; anyone alone joins their second-weakest practice. Participants without data choose a group by interest.

### 0:26-0:38 | Investigate: Hypothesize Together

- Each person's top selection in turn: first state what the data shows (the group asks clarifying questions only), then interpret it together: how I taught it, what worked, how other curricula teach it. The group names a hypothesis, and the teacher decides whether to keep it.
- Engagement: each group member leads the discussion of their own selection.
- Discussion: The report shows content and skills separately. Why treat the place where they meet as a hypothesis rather than an answer?

### 0:38-0:44 | Respond: Plan

- Complete the worksheet's action plan for your top hypothesis, fitted to your adopted curriculum. The group gives feedback on each plan.
- Discussion: What will you change in your teaching, and why do you expect it to help?

### 0:44-0:56 | Respond: Test

- Build an AP Classroom formative assessment by filtering the question bank by learning objective, skill, or content area. Group members share searches for their common skill; each builds for their own content focus.
- Schedule it before and/or after the change, and decide what result would mean reteaching. Its purpose is to test the hypothesis, not to serve as a grade. Participants without data draft an assessment for the group's hypothesis.
- Discussion: Why give a formative assessment instead of waiting for next year's scores?

### 0:56-1:00 | Close: Where You Go From Here

- Adjust happens after today: when results come back, reteach or spiral, and carry the results into future planning. An assessment is formative only when its results change what you teach.
- Groups trade contact information if they'd like to compare results later. The cycle works best with ongoing collaboration in a PLC, community of practice, CSTA chapter, or online group.
- Point to the take-home resources. Reflection: "By our definition, are you data-driven now?"

## Discussion Questions

In session:

- Why start with a question instead of opening the detailed report? (Notice: Ask)
- The report shows content and skills separately. Why treat the place where they meet as a hypothesis rather than an answer? (Hypothesize)
- What will you change in your teaching, and why do you expect it to help? (Plan)
- Why give a formative assessment instead of waiting for next year's scores? (Test)
- By our definition, are you data-driven now? (close)

Take-home prompts:

- What did your 3s learn that your 2s did not? (College Board's recommended comparison of two slices)
- What will you do if the formative results don't support your hypothesis?
- Who will you share your formative results with when they come back?

## Participant Outcomes

Participants leave with a hypothesis drawn from their AP results, an action plan, and a scheduled AP Classroom formative assessment. Tangible: slicing handout, browser tool, CSA and CSP worksheets, AP Classroom assessment handout. Intangible: confidence to repeat the cycle each year.

## Participant Resources

- **Slicing and download handout:** how to read the Subject Score Roster, turn what you see into a question (with sample questions and examples of questions the report can't answer), filter the Instructional Planning Report by score band and section to match the question, and download it.
- **Browser tool and documentation:** a free, privacy-focused tool that combines the report's test forms into two tables, units and practices, compared with global performance. Reports are processed in the browser and never uploaded. It works with the current report format.
- **Data Deep Dive worksheets, CSA and CSP versions:** each course's matrix of units and practices with sample skills in every cell, guidance on preferring earlier units, prerequisite checks, and an action-plan template.
- **AP Classroom formative assessment handout:** how to build an assessment by filtering the question bank by learning objective, skill, or content area; how to administer it; and how students and teachers see the results in AP Classroom.

Participants use each resource during the session and take all four home to repeat the cycle each year.
