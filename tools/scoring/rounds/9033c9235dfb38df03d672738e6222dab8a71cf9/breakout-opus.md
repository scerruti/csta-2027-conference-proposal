**1. CSTA Standards: 4 (identified, mostly relevant, minor areas for improvement)**
- "CSTA 4f, Plan instruction to foster student understanding: Step 4 (0:11–0:31) and Step 6 (0:31–0:43)"
- "CSTA 4g, Inform instruction through assessment: Steps 2, 4, and 6 (0:05–0:43)"

All three standards name their indicator and point to a timed segment, so all three count. 4f and 4g fit the session closely. 5f is a weak fit: teachers build an assessment, but nothing in the session shows how students will use feedback. No Student Subtopics are named.

**2. Content: 4 (three of four clearly present)**
- "☒ Session - 1 hour"
- "circles four low-performing cells on the Data Deep Dive worksheet"
- "Which part of your own AP data surprised you, and what would you need to check before believing it?"

(a) The official template is used, (b) learning goals, discussion questions and activities are all detailed, and (c) the activities suit a session. (d) is not clearly met. The 20-minute Step 4 block asks each person to set up a data slice, export from College Board, import into the tool, compare to benchmarks, circle cells, regroup and run dependency checks. That is too much for 20 minutes to count as clearly achievable.

**3. Educational Best Practices: 5 (three or more, each grounded)**
- "grounded in four steps of the Data Wise Improvement Process (Harvard Graduate School of Education)"
- "formative assessment, which uses classroom evidence to adjust instruction (Black and Wiliam, 1998)"
- "concept-level diagnosis... (Cabo, 2015)"

Under the counting rule, the four Data Wise steps count as one practice. With formative assessment and concept-level diagnosis, that makes three distinct practices, each with a citation. This is the top tier. The only soft spot is that the Cabo finding comes from a first Java course and is applied to AP CSA.

**4. Engaging and Relevance: 4 (engaging, relevant, fits grade level)**
- "Each participant with College Board access works with their own Instructional Planning Report data"
- "aggregates reports locally without uploading student information"
- "Participants without data join a group as supporters"

Working on your own real data, with a privacy-safe tool, is relevant and practical for AP CS teachers. It falls short of "highly engaging" because people without data play a supporting role. The process is a standard improvement cycle applied to AP data rather than something new.

**5. Takeaways: 4 (clear, tangible and intangible, resources detailed)**
- "Attendees take home all five resources"
- "one page defining score band, benchmark, test form, and prerequisite dependency"
- "Intangible: confidence to diagnose gaps with colleagues."

The tangible resources are described in detail, including what is on the handouts. The intangible side is a single short line, so the takeaways are not "extremely clear" on both sides.

**6. Attendee Preparedness: 4 (prepared, better CS teachers)**
- "Every participant takes home the action-plan template."
- "step-by-step instructions for setting up a data slice and exporting... and handling a failed export"
- "What would you need from your department to run this cycle with your own students?"

Practising the full cycle on their own data, then taking home a template and an export guide, prepares attendees well. It stops short of "extremely prepared" because supporters never run the export or analysis themselves, and each group plans for only one action area.

**7. Overall Recommendation: 4 (recommend acceptance, top 25%)**
- "The workflow follows Data Wise (Steps 2, 4, 6, and 7)."
- "groups check each other's indicators for measurability"

The proposal is well aligned, practical and well resourced. The tight Step 4 timing and the weak fit of 5f keep it out of the top 5%.

**What I could not judge from the text alone**
- Whether the live College Board login, export and import can be done in 20 minutes on conference Wi-Fi.
- How many attendees will actually have College Board access, which decides how many people are only supporters.
- Whether the Cabo (2015) paper supports the concept-level claim. Its cited title is about Bloom's taxonomy categories.
- Whether the browser tool works and protects privacy as described. Per the rubric, I scored it only on its description.
- The target grade level. AP implies high school, but the proposal never says so.
- What the companion mini-session covers, and whether this session really stands alone.
